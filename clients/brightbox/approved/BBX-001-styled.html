01 — The starting point

What a Real Small Budget Account Looks Like

Describing a bidding change now scheduled, Google writes: "If your campaign's Target CPA is $10, but your recent actual CPA performance is $5, your campaign will deliver more closely to a $10 actual CPA starting August 17, 2026." That is a cost per lead potentially doubling, and it lands hardest on advertisers with the least room to absorb it.

So the question business owners ask me is fair. If money is tight, is this worth starting at all?

Here is a real answer instead of a general one. I run a search campaign for a handyman in the Fort Wayne area. Last month it spent $440.53 and produced 12 leads at $36.72 each, which is a conversion rate just under 11%.

Not all of those leads turn into work, and I want to be honest about that. Some are price shoppers and some are jobs he does not take. But for a handyman, one decent remodel job pays for the whole month several times over.

That is what a small budget looks like when the settings are right.

02 — The threshold question

So Is $10 or $20 a Day Enough?

It depends on three things, and anyone who gives you a flat number without asking about them is guessing.

  • 01 How competitive your service is here. Fort Wayne is not Indianapolis or Chicago. In lower competition categories a modest daily budget goes further than most national advice suggests. In crowded ones it does not.
  • 02 What a job is worth to you. At $36.72 per lead, a business closing one in four is paying roughly $150 per customer. If your average job is $200, that is uncomfortable. If it is $2,000, it is excellent. The same figure is a good deal or a bad one depending entirely on your margins.
  • 03 What clicks cost in your category. In the account above they average $4.00. Higher value trades usually cost more, emergency services more again. Your ad budget buys a very different number of visitors depending on where you sit.

Quick tip Divide your monthly ad budget by your typical cost per click. That is roughly how many visitors you are buying. Under 50 a month is too thin to learn anything useful from.

03 — The real problem

Why Most Small Budgets Underperform

Here is the part that surprises people. When these campaigns fail, the money is usually not the reason. The settings are.

I looked back at the last 10 Google Ads accounts I have taken over or audited:

  • 8 / 10 had the Display Network and Search Partners switched on
  • 7 / 10 sent ad traffic to their homepage instead of a relevant page
  • 7 / 10 had no working conversion tracking at all
  • 6 / 10 were marked "Limited by budget"
  • 2 / 10 had no negative keywords set up

That is a small sample and I am not claiming it represents every account in the country. But the pattern has been consistent enough that I check these five things first, every time.

Problem 01
01

Display and Search Partners are on by default

When someone sets up search ads without changing the defaults, Google often includes the Display Network and Search Partners. Your ad ends up on third party sites and apps rather than in front of someone actively searching for your service. On a small budget that spend is the difference between getting leads and not.

Problem 02
02

Ad traffic goes to the homepage

Someone searching "handyman near me" clicks your ad and lands on a homepage describing everything you do. They now have to hunt for what they already told Google they wanted. Sending that click to a landing page about the specific service is one of the cheapest improvements available, and it costs nothing in ad spend.

Problem 03
03

Conversion tracking is missing or broken

This one matters most. Seven of those accounts could not tell you which clicks turned into calls or form submissions. Without that, Google's bidding has nothing to learn from and you cannot separate a good keyword from a bad one. You are not running a campaign at that point, you are buying visits.

Check rather than assume. In one account I reviewed, the conversion action was marked Primary but sat there Inactive and misconfigured, recording zero conversions while the campaign kept spending.

04 — Dated and confirmed

What Changes on August 17, 2026

This one is specific, and it only affects some campaigns.

Google is changing how target based bid strategies behave in campaigns marked "Limited by budget." Campaigns currently beating their target may drift back toward it, so a cost per lead that has been better than your target could rise.

Two conditions must both be true for this to reach you. The campaign has to show "Limited by budget", and it has to use a target based bid strategy such as Target CPA or Target ROAS. If you run Maximize conversions with no target set, it does not apply.

Six of those 10 accounts are limited by budget, so it is worth checking. Google released a Bid Target Adjustment Tool on July 6, 2026 for reviewing targets ahead of the date.

Important Nobody can tell you how much any individual account will move, including me. Google has not published that, and it will vary. What I can tell you is which accounts are exposed, and that checking takes a few minutes.

05 — My approach

How I Run a Small Budget Differently

When the budget is genuinely small, I stop trying to compete for the expensive terms and go narrow instead.

Longer tail keywords. Fewer people search them, so fewer advertisers bid on them and the clicks cost less. Keyword Planner will show you the search volume on these, and the low numbers put most advertisers off, which is exactly why they stay affordable. With the handyman client we could not chase big remodel keywords, because competition is heavy and those clicks are expensive. So we went after smaller, more specific jobs instead. Those clients often come back later with the bigger work.

A tighter radius. A small budget spread across a wide area buys almost nothing anywhere. Narrowing the target location concentrates it where the work actually is, which for most of my clients means Fort Wayne and the surrounding towns rather than the whole state.

A sniper approach on keyword research. I would rather show up reliably for 20 terms that describe exactly what my client does than appear occasionally for 200 that roughly relate. Exact match and phrase match keywords, grouped into tight ad groups, plus a real negative keyword list, do most of that work.

06 — The honest answer

When I Tell People Not to Run Google Ads

Sometimes Google is the wrong platform, and it is usually about intent.

Google works because people are already searching for your service. They have the problem right now. If your service is more of a nice to have, something people would not think to search for, Meta ads often work better. Someone scrolling Facebook sees it and thinks that would be nice, which is a different kind of demand.

The other option worth considering is Local Services Ads, where you pay per lead rather than per click. I have run these for a few clients rather than dozens, so I will not overclaim. But for a small budget service business, paying only when someone contacts you is worth looking at, and it is something I plan to test with my handyman client.

07 — Common questions

Frequently Asked Questions

Is $500 a month enough for Google Ads?

It can be. The account above spent about $440 and produced 12 leads. Whether that works for you depends on your cost per click and what a customer is worth.

Why does my campaign say "Limited by budget"?

Your daily budget is below what Google estimates is needed to capture the available clicks. It does not mean the campaign is failing, only that you are leaving impressions on the table.

What is the difference between budget and bid?

Your budget is what you spend per day. Your bid is what you pay for one click. A thin budget paired with an unrealistically low bid usually means your ads simply do not show.

Do I really need conversion tracking?

Especially when spending is limited. With less to spend you can afford waste even less, and without tracking you cannot tell which keywords produce work.

Should I use Performance Max?

I am cautious with it when spending is tight. It needs conversion data to learn from, and smaller accounts often do not have enough yet.

The Short Version

A small Google Ads budget can absolutely work. The handyman account proves it at $15 a day. What it cannot survive is default settings, missing conversion tracking and traffic pointed at a homepage.

Before you add money, check what your current spend is actually buying. In most accounts I see, there is more to recover from fixing settings than from increasing the ad budget.
