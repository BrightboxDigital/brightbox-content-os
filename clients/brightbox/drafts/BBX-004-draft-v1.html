01 — The framework

The Three Factors Google Names

Google Business Profile, still called Google My Business by some older tools and articles, is the free listing that controls how your business shows up on Google Search and Maps. Search "Google Business Profile ranking factors" and you'll find plenty of agencies handing out invented percentage breakdowns, 34% this, 21% that, with no methodology behind the number. Google has never published a weighting like that. What it has published is simpler and worth working from directly: its own explanation of how local results are ranked, built on three named factors.

"Relevance is how well a Business Profile matches what someone is searching for. Distance refers to how far each business is from the customer who's searching. Prominence means how well-known a business is."

That's it. No fourth factor, no secret formula. The rest of this article is about what moves each one, in plain terms, and what a lot of Fort Wayne business owners get wrong when they're managing their own profile.

Important Google is also direct about this: "There's no way to request or pay for a better local ranking on Google." If someone offers to buy or guarantee you a better spot in the local pack, the map results at the top of a local search, that's not something Google's own system allows for.

02 — In practice

What Moves Each Factor

Relevance

Relevance comes down to how well your profile matches what someone typed or said. The biggest lever here is your primary category, covered in its own section below, since it's the mistake we see most often. Beyond category, relevance is shaped by a complete, accurate business description, the services and products you've listed, and attributes that describe what you offer. A profile that's missing half its fields is harder for Google to match to a search, no matter how good the business behind it is.

Distance

Distance is exactly what it sounds like: how far a business is from the person searching. You can't move your business to change this, but you can make sure your address and service area are set correctly, and, for service-area businesses without a public storefront, that the service areas listed reflect where you work.

In my own experience managing profiles, distance is the factor I see move the needle the most. I can look at my own business, Brightbox Digital, and see it: for the keywords I want to rank for, I rank high in my immediate area, and the further out you get, the lower that ranking drops. I've watched the same pattern with a plumbing client here in Fort Wayne. He ranks well for his target keywords close to his service base, and that ranking fades the farther out you go. That's not a controlled test and Google doesn't publish a number to confirm it, but it's a consistent pattern across more than one account I manage.

Brightbox Perspective If a client asks me which factor matters most, distance is my honest answer, based on what I've seen managing profiles day to day, not a Google-published ranking of the three. Relevance and prominence still matter, they're just the two you have more control over.

Prominence

Prominence is how well known your business is, both on Google and off it. Google's own documentation on this factor points to review quantity and ratings, and to links and citations pointing to your business from elsewhere on the web. A business with more reviews, better ratings, and a real presence beyond its own profile tends to read as more prominent than one that looks the same on paper but has none of that.

03 — The most common mistake

The One Thing Most Owners Get Wrong First

When I audit a new client's profile, this is the first thing I check, and it's the single most common mistake I find: the wrong primary category.

Your primary category should be your highest-value service, the one you most want to win business for, not just the most general description of what you do. Google offers thousands of business categories, and most businesses genuinely fit more than one. Secondary categories exist to cover everything else.

Example A remodeling client of mine does kitchens, bathrooms, and general handyman work. If "Handyman" is set as the primary category, the profile is optimized to rank for handyman searches first, not bathroom remodels, even though bathroom remodels are the higher-value service he wants more of. The fix is choosing the primary category based on what you most want to sell, and using secondary categories to cover the rest, not the other way around.

This one setting shapes both how often you show up for the searches you care about and which services you end up selling more of. It's worth getting right before touching anything else on the profile.

04 — What changed

Reviews Just Got a Real Rulebook

Prominence is partly built on reviews, which makes how you ask for them worth getting right. Google's Business Profile content policy is explicit about what crosses the line, and some of it is stricter than a lot of business owners realize.

Prohibited, in Google's own words: businesses that "discourage or prohibit negative reviews, or selectively solicit positive reviews from customers," merchants "requesting that staff solicit a certain number of reviews," merchants "requesting that staff solicit reviews that include specific content, including content that identifies a staff member," and requiring or pressuring customers to leave a review while they're still on the premises.

Important That second one catches more businesses than they expect. A tech asking a customer "did you have a good experience? If so, would you leave us a review?" is textbook selective solicitation, asking only the customers who already signaled a good outcome. It doesn't need to happen on the job site to count. The same ask over email or text is just as much a violation.

What's still fine, straight from the same policy: soliciting or encouraging "the posting of content that does represent a genuine experience, without offering incentives to do so or attempting to influence the rating or the contents of the review." In plain terms, ask every customer, not just the ones you expect a good answer from, and don't offer anything in exchange.

Brightbox Perspective I've had clients using exactly this kind of selective ask and had to walk it back with them. The fix isn't complicated: ask everyone for honest feedback, the same way, every time. Don't filter who gets asked based on how the conversation went.

05 — A common question

Does Posting on Your Profile Help You Rank?

Short answer: not according to anything Google has published, and not according to what I've seen managing client profiles. Google's own help page for posts describes them as a way to "share up-to-date info," "promote your sales, specials, events, news, and offers," and "connect with your customers." Ranking isn't mentioned anywhere on that page.

I don't post to client profiles constantly, and I don't believe it moves the ranking needle. What it does do is show anyone looking at the profile that the business is active and paying attention. That's a real reason to keep it up, just not the reason most people assume.

06 — Worth checking

Common Mistakes That Hurt Visibility

These three show up on business profiles across Fort Wayne more than almost anything else, and each one is fixable in an afternoon.

  • 01Incomplete information. A missing hours field, an outdated phone number, or no website link makes a profile look less trustworthy to both customers and to Google's own matching, and it's an easy fix that gets skipped constantly.
  • 02Ignoring reviews entirely, good and bad. A profile with reviews nobody ever responds to reads as unmanaged, and an unanswered negative review sits there indefinitely with no context added.
  • 03Underusing the profile's own features. Products, services, attributes, and business description fields all feed relevance directly, and a profile that only has the bare minimum filled in is leaving straightforward, no-cost improvement on the table.

07 — Getting it right

Getting Your Profile Set Up Right

If you're setting up a profile from scratch or auditing an existing one, this is the short version. Choose your primary category based on the service you most want to sell, then add secondary categories for everything else you do. Verify your business so the listing can go live and you get full access to every feature. Fill in every field you have real information for: hours, phone number, website, service areas, business description, products, and services. Then keep it current as any of that changes.

That's the foundation the rest of this article builds on. Get the primary category and complete information right first, and the rest of what affects your visibility becomes easier to manage.

08 — Common questions

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I get my Google Business Profile to rank higher?

Start with the primary category, since it's the most common thing set wrong. From there, complete every field with accurate information, and build genuine reviews by asking every customer the same way, not just the ones you expect a good answer from.

Can a service-area business without a public address rank well?

Yes. Google has a specific setup path for service-area businesses, including how to hide your address and list the areas you serve. Getting that setup right matters more for a business like this than for one with a public storefront.

Does posting on my Google Business Profile help my ranking?

Not according to anything Google has published. Posts are a way to show activity and share updates, not a documented ranking factor.

Is it still okay to ask customers for reviews?

Yes, as long as you ask everyone the same way and don't offer anything in exchange. What's not okay is asking only customers who already said they had a good experience, requesting a specific number from staff, or pressuring someone on-site to leave one.

Why does the primary category matter so much?

It's the main signal Google uses to match your profile to a search. The wrong primary category means you're optimized to show up for the wrong service, even if everything else on the profile is filled in correctly.

Where This Leaves You

Relevance, distance, and prominence are the whole framework, straight from Google. Most of the real, controllable wins live inside relevance and prominence: the right primary category, complete information, and honest reviews asked for the same way every time. Distance is worth understanding but not something you can change by editing your profile. Most struggling business profiles are missing one of these, not losing to some unbeatable local search competitor. If you've been putting off a proper look at your own profile, especially the category question, that's the one place to start.
